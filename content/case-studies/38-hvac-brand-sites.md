## The client, in the terms I can share

An HVAC consolidator group: a company that acquires established heating, cooling, and plumbing contractors across a region, keeps the local brand names customers already trust, and operates them as a portfolio. Thirty-eight brands, each with its own domain, its own service pages, its own reviews, and its own local reputation.

The client is not named here. The number is the point, not the logo. Results are reported as a percentage at the client's request.

## The problem consolidators inherit

Roll-ups happen fast. What gets acquired is a company that is good at installing furnaces — not a company that is good at running a website. So the portfolio arrives with a predictable set of defects:

- Dozens of sites built by dozens of different agencies, or by a cousin, or by nobody
- Service pages that duplicate each other across brands in overlapping metros
- Redirect chains left over from old domain changes, thin location pages, missing structured data
- Analytics that do not line up between brands, so no one can say which markets are actually growing
- Conversion problems nobody owns: forms that ask for too much, phone numbers that are not tracked, pages written for people who already know the brand

None of it is dramatic on its own. All of it compounds — because in a portfolio, the same defect repeats across every brand that inherited it. A broken redirect is an annoyance. The same broken redirect pattern on twenty sites is a ceiling.

## What I did

### 1. Took single ownership of all 38 websites

Before any tactic, one person had to be accountable for the whole surface. Distributed ownership is how portfolios end up with 38 half-finished roadmaps and no decisions. One owner means one backlog, one set of standards, and someone who can say no.

### 2. Built one content strategy, executed at brand level

Not 38 strategies. One architecture applied 38 times: which page types earn a place on every domain, which queries each brand should own, where metros overlap, and how each brand differentiates instead of competing with its own siblings for the same search.

This is the piece consolidators usually skip. They treat each acquired site as an independent marketing problem, so the group ends up bidding against itself in the same city for the same service.

### 3. Fixed the technical floor

Redirect chains, broken canonicals, orphaned service pages, duplicate titles, missing structured data, sitemaps that did not match reality. Unglamorous work, and most of the time it is the highest-leverage block on the list — because every hour of content and every dollar of ad spend lands on top of it.

### 4. Rebuilt internal linking on purpose

Service pages, location pages, and supporting content have to point at each other deliberately. Internal links are how you tell a search engine which page matters most and how it should understand the relationship between a service and a market. Most consolidated sites never say anything — they link in whatever order the theme happened to output.

### 5. Improved CRO on the pages that already had traffic

Lead forms, phone prominence, service page structure, trust signals, and the friction between "interested" and "submitted." Pages that already rank are the cheapest growth available to you. Rewriting a page nobody reaches is busywork.

## The result

**Organic leads, FY2024 to FY2025: +78% year over year.**

I report leads rather than sessions for a reason. Traffic is a means; sessions can climb while leads fall if you are ranking for the wrong things. Leads are the number the owner of the business actually cares about, and it is the number that survives scrutiny.

## Why it worked

**Sequencing.** Technical floor first, then content, then conversion on pages that already had demand. Fixing conversion on a page nobody reaches is wasted effort. Publishing content onto a broken site wastes the content. Most portfolios run all five workstreams at once and wonder why the graph stays flat.

**Portfolio-level strategy, brand-level execution.** The unit of thinking was the group. The unit of doing was the individual brand and its market.

**One accountable owner.** Thirty-eight sites, one person responsible, one standard applied everywhere. Consolidation only pays off when the marketing is consolidated too.

## How to tell whether you have this problem

If you run more than one brand site, check these honestly:

☐ You cannot name who owns each of your websites

☐ Two of your brands rank for the same service in the same metro

☐ Your highest-traffic pages have not been touched in over a year

☐ You have traffic reporting but no per-brand lead reporting

☐ Your redirects still point at URLs from the last migration

☐ No one has audited internal links on any of your sites

Three or more, and the growth is already sitting in the portfolio — it just is not being collected.

## If you are not a consolidator

The same playbook compresses down to one site: audit before you spend, fix the technical floor, be deliberate about internal links, then improve conversion on pages that already get traffic.

Order beats effort. That is most of it.
